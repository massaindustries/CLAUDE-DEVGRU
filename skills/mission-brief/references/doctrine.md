# Mission Brief — Doctrine Reference

## Origin
SMEAC is the five-paragraph operations order (OPORD) format codified by the United States Marine Corps and adopted across NATO ground forces. It distills any combat order into five sections that, when read aloud, leave no ambiguity about what's happening, who does what, with what, and how everyone stays in sync.

The mnemonic is taught in The Basic School (Quantico, Virginia) to every newly commissioned Marine officer and has been refined since the early 20th century. The same format appears in the U.S. Army's FM 6-0 (*Commander and Staff Organization and Operations*, Annex A) and in NATO's STANAG 2014 Edition 9. U.S. Naval Special Warfare adopts the format with maritime/small-unit additions in NWP 3-05.2 and the *SEAL Patrol Leader's Handbook*.

## Canonical Sub-Paragraphs of the Five-Paragraph OPORD
Naïve SMEAC is "five headings"; doctrinal SMEAC is dozens of named sub-paragraphs, each surfacing a specific failure class. Per FM 6-0 Annex A:

**1. Situation**
- 1a. Area of Interest
- 1b. Area of Operations (1b(1) Terrain — OAKOC; 1b(2) Weather)
- 1c. Enemy Forces — composition, disposition, strength, *most likely* and *most dangerous* COAs
- 1d. Friendly Forces — higher's mission and intent two levels up; adjacent/supporting units
- 1e. Interagency, Intergovernmental, NGO actors
- 1f. Civil Considerations — ASCOPE (Areas, Structures, Capabilities, Organizations, People, Events)
- 1g. Attachments and Detachments
- 1h. Assumptions (carried in OPLANs; replaced by facts as info arrives)

**2. Mission** — single concise sentence; no sub-paragraphs. Five Ws + "in order to" purpose.

**3. Execution** — opens with **Commander's Intent**, then:
- 3a. Concept of Operations
- 3b. Scheme of Movement and Maneuver
- 3c. Scheme of Fires
- 3d. Scheme of Protection / Engineer / Intelligence sub-schemes
- 3e. Stability Tasks
- 3f. Assessment (MOEs / MOPs)
- 3g. Tasks to Subordinate Units (each task + purpose)
- 3h. Coordinating Instructions — CCIR (PIR + FFIR), EEFI, fire support coordination measures, ROE, risk-reduction, **branches and abort criteria**

**4. Sustainment / Admin & Logistics** — Logistics (Classes I–X) / Personnel / Health Service Support.

**5. Command and Signal** — Command (location, succession), Control (CP, reports), Signal (SOI, **PACE plan**, code words, recognition signals).

The point of memorizing the canonical sub-paragraphs is not to fill them all every time — it's to know what you're choosing to skip when you do.

## The WARNO → OPORD → FRAGO Lifecycle
Per FM 6-0 chapter 9:

- **WARNO (Warning Order)** — preliminary notice. Issued immediately on receipt of mission and at each MDMP milestone. Triggers **parallel planning** by subordinates. May omit any paragraph for which no information yet exists. *Anticipate.*
- **OPORD (Operation Order)** — full five-paragraph order. Issued no later than ⅓ of available time before execution (the 1/3-2/3 rule). Replaces the most recent OPLAN/WARNO and becomes the base order. *Commit.*
- **FRAGO (Fragmentary Order)** — abbreviated change. Contains **only the deltas** from the base order; unaddressed paragraphs remain in force. Keyed to the parent OPORD. *Adapt.*

Lifecycle: **anticipate (WARNO) → commit (OPORD) → adapt (FRAGO)**.

## Mission Statement Construction
Doctrinal formula (FM 6-0 para 9-24; ADP 5-0 para 2-90):

> `[UNIT] [TASK] [OBJECT] [TIME] [LOCATION] in order to [PURPOSE]`

The task uses approved tactical-task vocabulary (FM 3-90 Appendix B). The "in order to" clause links the task to the higher commander's purpose. The mission statement is the *only* paragraph the commander writes personally (FM 6-0 para 9-26).

Real examples:

1. *"1st Brigade attacks 060500ZJUN to seize OBJECTIVE LION (vic NB123456) **in order to** prevent enemy reinforcement of Phase Line BLUE."* (FM 6-0 sample order)
2. *"Allied Expeditionary Force will enter the continent of Europe and, in conjunction with the other United Nations, undertake operations aimed at the heart of Germany and the destruction of her armed forces, **in order to** bring about the unconditional surrender of Germany."* (SHAEF Directive, 12 Feb 1944)
3. *"VII Corps attacks 240400CFEB91 to destroy the Republican Guard Forces Command in zone **in order to** prevent reconstitution of Iraqi offensive capability."* (ARCENT OPORD, Desert Storm)

## Commander's Intent — Three Components
Per ADP 5-0 para 2-95 and ADP 6-0 para 1-26:

1. **Expanded Purpose** — *why* of the operation, broader than the mission's "in order to" clause. Frames the op against the higher fight.
2. **Key Tasks** — activities the force must perform *as a whole* to achieve the end state. **Survive a COA change** — if the original plan becomes invalid, key tasks still get done.
3. **End State** — observable conditions when the operation is complete, expressed in terms of friendly forces, enemy, terrain, and civil considerations.

Civilian adopters treat commander's intent as a "vision statement." Doctrinally it is a **decision-aid for disobedience** — what subordinates use to act *two echelons down* when the order no longer fits reality (MCDP 1 *Warfighting*, ch. 4, "Mission Tactics"). FM 6-0 para 9-29 requires intent be brief enough to be understood and remembered two echelons below the issuing commander.

## The Three-Checkpoint Verification Chain
Per FM 6-0 chapter 12 and CALL Newsletter 98-5, three distinct verification mechanisms, ordered:

| Mechanism | When | What it verifies |
|---|---|---|
| **Confirmation Brief** | Immediately after the OPORD is issued | "Did the subordinate *hear and understand* the order?" — restates intent, task, purpose, relation to others |
| **Back-Brief** | After subordinate's own planning, before execution | "Is the subordinate's *plan* consistent with intent?" — subordinate explains *how* they will execute |
| **Rehearsal** | Before execution | "Does the *combined* plan work in time and space?" — multilateral synchronization |

Each verifies a different failure mode. Skipping confirmation brief → subordinate plans on wrong premises. Skipping back-brief → bad plan only discovered at rehearsal. Skipping rehearsal → discovered in contact (catastrophic).

NSW failure indicators for back-brief (per the *SEAL Patrol Leader's Handbook* and FM 6-0 App F): subordinate parrots the order verbatim (memorization, not internalization), cannot articulate the *why* / purpose, cannot describe branch decisions. Any of these → re-issue the brief or replace the operator.

## Tier-1 SOF Additions
The five-paragraph order is conventional doctrine. Tier-1 SOF communities each layer a *forced-dissent mechanism* on top — the structural recognition that the brief's author has blind spots the audience can see.

- **SAS — "Chinese Parliament"** (origin: David Stirling, L Detachment, Western Desert 1941-43). After the formal Patrol Order, every member — trooper through officer — is required to challenge the plan with specific objections. Rank is suspended for the duration. Session ends when no member has an unaddressed concern. Then locked.
- **Delta Force — "Murder Board"** (per Haney, *Inside Delta Force*; Pushies, *US Counter-Terrorist Forces*). Pre-mission peer attack on every assumption. Operators write on the plan in front of the squadron commander, who signs the result.
- **JSOC — Brief-Back to Commander** (per McChrystal, *My Share of the Task*). 5-15 minute compressed brief-back. The commander asks three questions: *What can kill us? What can we not control? What is your abort trigger?*
- **IDF Sayeret Matkal — Mutual Back-Briefing**. Each team leader briefs the *neighboring* team's plan, surfacing seam failures.

All four are the same control surface under different names: **forced surfacing of dissent before launch**.

## F3EAD as a Briefing Doctrine
Find, Fix, Finish, Exploit, Analyze, Disseminate. Articulated by Flynn and McChrystal in Iraq (TF-714, 2003-08). Each phase produces a **briefing artifact** that feeds the next:

- Find → target package
- Fix → strike window
- Finish → CONOP storyboard (one-page graphical OPORD)
- Exploit → SSE flash report
- Analyze → updated target deck
- Disseminate → next Find input

The discipline isn't the kill chain — it's that the briefing surface is *horizontal* (cross-task-force, cross-agency), not just *vertical*. Conventional intel briefs are upward-facing PowerPoint; F3EAD briefs are lateral and operator-facing.

## PACE Beyond Comms
PACE originated in U.S. military signal doctrine (FM 6-0 Annex H, STANAG 2014 para 5c) as **Primary / Alternate / Contingency / Emergency** — the order in which an element will move through available systems until contact is established. Each tier must be **diverse in failure mode** (different bearer, different infrastructure) so one failure does not cascade.

Doctrinal rule (FM 6-0 para H-7): a PACE must be ordered for **each critical information flow** — command, fires, intelligence, sustainment, MEDEVAC — not for "comms" as a single bucket.

**Generalization beyond comms:** PACE is a redundancy doctrine for any single-point-of-failure capability. U.S. SOF and joint planners apply it to extraction (primary HLZ, alternate HLZ, contingency overland, emergency E&E), to ISR feeds, to GPS/PNT (GPS → terrestrial radionav → celestial → dead-reckoning), and to logistics nodes. The principle: **if it must work, you owe it four independent paths.**

## The 1/3 – 2/3 Rule
FM 6-0 para 2-86 (also FM 5-0 and FM 7-0; traceable to FM 101-5 / CGSC Staff Officers' Handbook):

> *The commander uses no more than one-third of available time before execution for own/staff planning; at least two-thirds is reserved for subordinates to plan, rehearse, conduct PCC/PCI, and rest.*

Subordinates have their own troop-leading cycle. Time consumed at the higher echelon compounds downward — every hour the battalion spends planning costs companies, platoons, and squads an hour each. The bottleneck on mission readiness is rarely the higher HQ's plan; it's the lowest echelon's rehearsal and preparation time.

## Three Real-World Case Studies

### Operation Eagle Claw (Desert One, April 1980) — *Negative*
The Holloway Report (1980) identified 23 deficiencies. The briefing-and-rehearsal failures were the central ones:
- **No full joint dress rehearsal** — Delta at Fort Bragg, helo crews at Yuma, fixed-wing at Hurlburt, never together. Excessive OPSEC compartmentation drove the seam.
- **No common command brief** — inter-service trust never built; crews and ground force met for the first time at the objective.
- **Inadequate contingency planning** — the minimum-mission-capable helo threshold (6) had no branch for falling below it.

**Direct result:** USSOCOM stood up in 1987; joint mission rehearsals became mandatory.

### Battle of Mogadishu (Task Force Ranger, 3–4 Oct 1993) — *Negative*
Per the DTIC monograph *Anatomy of a Failure* (ADA366316) and the declassified Task Force Ranger Operations report:
- **Pattern-of-life violation in planning** — six prior raids had used near-identical TTPs; the brief never updated to account for enemy learning (RPGs being used as anti-aircraft after the 15 Sept shoot-down).
- **No branch for downed helicopter** despite a known threat.
- **QRF improvised post-brief** — no armor, no AC-130 requested.
- **Overconfidence in the brief itself** — *"we've done this six times"* replaced disciplined contingency planning.

### Operation Neptune Spear (May 2011) — *Positive*
Per Owen / Bissonnette, *No Easy Day*:
- **Two full-scale mock-up rehearsals** — one at Harvey Point, NC; one in Nevada.
- **Multiple PACE branches** built into the plan.
- **The helo crash on the X** was immediately handled because *"land in the courtyard if the wall hits"* had been **rehearsed as a contingency**. The failure mode was pre-briefed, so it just executed.

The contrast across these three teaches the same lesson three different ways: the brief that survives contact is the one whose **assumptions are tied to abort triggers** and whose **branches are rehearsed, not just listed**.

## AI-Agent Worked Example
A complete brief for a real Claude Code task, every sub-bucket filled. This is what SOTA execution looks like.

**Task**: *"Refactor the auth module to use session cookies instead of JWT-in-localStorage."*

```
# MISSION BRIEF — Auth: JWT-in-localStorage → session cookies

## S — Situation
- Specified: replace JWT-in-localStorage with session cookies in the auth module
- Implied: server-side session store, CSRF protection, migration path for existing logged-in users, mobile client compatibility
- Essential: zero forced re-auth at deploy; CSRF protection on all mutating routes; mobile client continues working
- Constraints / Restrictions: no schema breaking changes on the users table; deploy must be reversible within 24h
- Assumptions:
  - Existing session store (Redis) has capacity for active-user count | ABORT TRIGGER: capacity check fails or eviction rate > 5%/min
  - Mobile clients can be updated to send the session cookie in their next release | ABORT TRIGGER: native client cannot ship within deploy window
  - CSRF middleware already exists in framework | ABORT TRIGGER: framework lacks built-in CSRF, would require auth-adjacent rewrite

## M — Mission
Backend team refactors `auth/` to issue HTTP-only session cookies (Redis-backed) by EOW on branch `feat/session-cookies`, in order to remove the XSS-exfiltration risk of JWT-in-localStorage and prepare for SSO integration in Q3.

## E — Execution
**Commander's Intent**
- Expanded Purpose: kill the most-exploited class of frontend token-exfil vector; lay the foundation for the Q3 SSO rollout
- Key Tasks: cookie-based auth flow, CSRF protection on all mutating routes, rollback path that preserves user sessions
- End State: zero JWTs in localStorage in production traffic; all mutating routes 403 on missing/invalid CSRF; rollback to JWT possible by feature flag within 60 seconds

**Tasks**
1. Add session-cookie issuance in login/refresh handlers in order to establish the new auth path
2. Add CSRF middleware to all non-GET routes in order to close the cookie-attack surface
3. Add a feature flag (`AUTH_MODE=cookie|jwt`) in order to enable instant rollback
4. Update mobile API contract docs and ship native release in order to keep mobile working
5. Run dual-issue (both cookie and JWT) for 48h, then deprecate JWT in order to drain logged-in users without forced logout

**Actions On / Branches**
- Redis capacity check shows eviction > 5%/min → SRE on-call → halt rollout, scale Redis, re-run capacity check
- Native mobile blocked by store review → mobile lead → keep dual-issue mode until mobile ships
- CSRF middleware breaks a non-mutating route mis-tagged as POST → me → audit route table, fix tag, redeploy
- Sentry shows > 0.5% increase in 401s post-deploy → me → flip feature flag back to JWT, file incident

**Commander's Three Questions**
- What can kill us: CSRF middleware misconfiguration causing a wave of 403s on legitimate traffic
- What we can't control: mobile App Store review timeline
- Abort trigger: > 5% Redis eviction rate, OR > 0.5% rise in 401s, OR mobile cannot ship within 7d

## A — Admin / Logistics
- Time / token budget (1/3-2/3): ~45 min brief + planning; ~6h execution; ~3h monitoring + verification + rollback rehearsal
- Resources: Redis session store, framework CSRF middleware, feature-flag service, Sentry, mobile release pipeline
- Dependencies: SRE sign-off on Redis capacity; mobile lead confirms native release slot; CSRF middleware audited (already done in prior PR)

## C — Command / Signal
- Reporting cadence: checkpoint after Task 1 (cookie issuance live behind flag), checkpoint after Task 3 (flag flips work in staging), checkpoint at 48h dual-issue boundary
- Done criteria: zero JWTs in production frontend storage (verified via observability); all non-GET routes 403 on missing CSRF (verified via test suite + canary); rollback verified in staging
- PACE (auth issuance): P=session cookie (Redis-backed) A=session cookie (in-memory fallback if Redis down) C=JWT (feature flag rollback) E=force-logout banner + read-only mode
```

This is what a doctrinally complete brief produces. The buckets are filled. The assumptions are tied to triggers. The branches have decision authorities. PACE applies to the actual must-work capability. The commander's three questions are answered honestly. A SOF reviewer would sign this.
